# Narrative, innovation and confidence in speculative bubbles

Status: research hypothesis, not universal causal law.

## Central hypothesis
Many speculative bubbles begin around genuine innovations, new economic opportunities or popular narratives. Prices and financing may then incorporate expectations that exceed demonstrable adoption, earnings, cash flows or sustainable collateral values. Subsequent expectation revisions, financing constraints and selling feedback can produce large drawdowns even when the underlying innovation succeeds.

## Separate dimensions
- Innovation or craze: dated product/industry emergence, adoption, coverage and investor participation.
- Expectations: contemporaneous forecasts, survey beliefs, valuation-implied growth and issuance narratives.
- Delivery: realized revenue, margins, cash flow, adoption and unit economics.
- Expectation-delivery gap: predeclared forecast error and valuation-implied requirements, with vintage and horizon.
- Amplification: margin debt, leverage, refinancing dependence, market breadth and concentration.
- Confidence revision: forecast downgrades, flow reversals, funding costs and changes in risk premia.
- Outcomes: specified index/sector drawdown, first crossing date, recovery and independently defined recession outcome.

## Candidate case families
Railway speculation, 1920s new industries, dot-com technology, housing-credit narratives, and digital-asset speculation. These are candidate episodes, not proof that every episode follows the same mechanism.

## Competing explanations
- A valuation decline can reflect higher discount rates even when earnings expectations remain stable.
- Liquidity or leverage can force selling before an observable change in technology beliefs.
- Some bubbles arise from credit, land or commodity dynamics without a novel invention.
- Some expensive innovations grow into prior valuations without a 20% index drawdown.
- Some narratives collapse because the promised product fails; others because prices were excessive despite product success.

## Tests
1. Define speculative valuation independently of eventual crashes.
2. Collect original dated forecasts and contemporaneous valuations, not retrospective success narratives.
3. Compare innovations that experienced large drawdowns with innovations that did not, using matched valuation, leverage and funding conditions.
4. Test whether expectation-delivery gaps precede confidence changes and drawdowns; distinguish correlation from causation.
5. Report non-events, missing observations, index choice, publication lags and external-shock sensitivity uniformly.
6. Do not assert a deterministic point of no return or claim all bubbles are technology driven.

## Initial deliverable
A historical episode matrix: narrative, contemporaneous expected outcome, observed delivery, valuation requirements, financing conditions, confidence revision evidence, drawdown outcome and sources.
